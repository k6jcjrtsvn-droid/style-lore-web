Color season

True Winter

Cool and clear, with striking, high-contrast coloring.

If you are a True Winter, you have probably put on a beige knit that looked lovely on the hanger and wondered why your face went tired, then noticed that never happens in plain black. That is the season in one moment: your coloring is cool, clear and sharply defined, and it wants colors that are just as decisive.

The typical True Winter has hair on the dark side, skin with a cool cast at any depth from porcelain to deep brown, and eyes that read clearly against both. Nothing in the face is blended or dusty, so anything blended or dusty on the body looks borrowed.

Let the pieces contrast. A light piece against a dark one matches the contrast already in your own coloring; an all-mid-tone outfit can look like it has gone slightly grey.

The three dials

Every season is a position on three dials. Hue is the undertone question: does a color lean toward gold or toward blue. Yours sits hard at the cool end. Blue-based reds, pinks and greens sit quietly on your skin; the moment gold creeps in, your skin looks sallow beside it.

Value is depth, how light or dark the overall impression is. True Winter sits in the middle to deep range, which is why the dial above reads medium rather than deep. You are not defined by darkness the way a Deep Winter is; you are defined by the fact that black and white both look at home on you, so the full range of values is open.

Chroma is clarity, how pure or how greyed a color is, and it is your loudest dial. True Winter colors are pure pigment with no grey stirred in. A dusty rose is not wrong in hue, it is wrong in chroma, and the effect is a smudge on a clean window. The fourth reading the quiz takes, contrast, follows from the other three: your features sit far apart in value, so your outfits can too.

Colors that work

Pure cool colors at full clarity — true red, royal blue, magenta, emerald, stark white and black.

Colors that fight it

Anything dusty, muted or warm-golden.

Your best neutrals

Stark neutrals: true black, charcoal, pure white, deep navy. Winter is the one family that can wear real black next to real white and look better for it.

  • True black — the anchor of the wardrobe. On you it sharpens the jaw and brightens the eyes rather than hollowing the face.
  • Pure white — optic, blue-white, not cream. Next to black it produces the exact contrast your own hair and skin already have.
  • Charcoal — the everyday black, especially for trousers and knitwear that will be worn with a bright.
  • Deep navy — the inkiest navy you can find. It does what black does with slightly less drama.
  • Cool mid-grey — wet slate rather than warm stone; your best neutral for a coat that has to sit next to many accents.
  • Icy grey — very pale grey with a hint of blue. This is your version of beige: the light neutral that does not turn your skin yellow.

Your best accents

These are the colors that make people ask whether you have changed something. Wear them as a whole piece, not a trim, against black, white or navy.

  • True red — neither orange nor wine, the red of a pillar box. The single most useful accent you own.
  • Royal blue and cobalt — clear, saturated blues that lift the whites of the eyes.
  • Magenta and fuchsia — blue-based pink at full volume. Where a Summer wears rose, you wear this.
  • Emerald — a clean green with blue underneath it, never olive or moss.
  • Icy pink and icy blue — the pale end of your palette: white with a drop of pigment, crisp rather than soft.
  • Icy lemon — a sharp yellow with no gold in it, best in small amounts under a dark jacket.

Colors to be careful with

Most True Winter mistakes are not about hue. They are about grey creeping into the color, or gold creeping into the neutral.

  • Beige, camel and tan — they warm the skin toward yellow and dull the eyes. Swap for icy grey, pure white or charcoal.
  • Dusty rose and mauve — right hue, wrong chroma; they make you look like you are recovering from something. Swap for icy pink or fuchsia.
  • Olive and khaki — warm and greyed, so they fail on two dials at once. Swap for emerald, pine or deep navy.
  • Mustard and gold-yellow — the fastest way to look sallow. Swap for icy lemon, or take the yellow out and use white.
  • Heathered greys and marl — the fuzzy grey of a gym sweatshirt reads dusty on you. A flat charcoal is the same idea in your chroma.

Metals, denim, black and white

Silver, platinum and white gold. Yellow gold usually reads slightly dirty against cool skin.

Denim. Dark, cool indigo is your denim, the kind that looks almost navy. Black denim and white denim work as well as anything you own. Skip the faded, yellowed vintage washes; a grey-cast wash is fine if it is crisp rather than muddy.

Black. Yes, without conditions. You are one of the few seasons for whom black is a flattering color rather than a safe one, and you can wear it head to toe.

White. Pure, optic white. Cream and ivory do to you what beige does, only more subtly, so you may not notice until you see a photo. If a white looks slightly grey on the hanger next to a warm white, that is your white.

Hair, makeup and glasses notes

Hair. Your natural hair is almost certainly cool: black, espresso, ash brown or, less often, ash blonde. If you color it, keep it cool: blue-black, ash, cool chestnut. A caramel or honey balayage pulls your skin toward yellow, and so do golden greys.

Makeup. Lips take true red, fuchsia and cool berry with ease; a clear red lip with almost nothing else on the face is a very True Winter look. Blush should be cool pink rather than peach. Black or navy eyeliner suits the contrast in your face; soft brown liner looks as though it is trying to fade you.

Glasses. Black, clear or crystal frames, silver, gunmetal and cobalt all sit well. Honey tortoiseshell fights your skin; if you love the pattern, look for a grey or black-and-clear tortoise instead.

A week of color combinations

Five pairings you can build a week around. Each keeps the contrast high and the colors pure.

  • Monday — black trousers, a pure white shirt, a true red scarf or lipstick. The season in one outfit.
  • Tuesday — deep navy with icy pink: a navy knit over an icy pink shirt, or the reverse.
  • Wednesday — charcoal and emerald. Charcoal trousers with an emerald top reads polished without a single warm note.
  • Thursday — cobalt with white and black in equal parts, for a day you want to be noticed.
  • Friday — black with magenta. One piece in magenta, everything else black, silver at the ears.

Most often confused with Bright Winter

Both cool and clear. True Winter is balanced — it can wear stark black and white as the whole outfit. Bright Winter wants the intensity turned all the way up and looks slightly flat in plain black and white.

Neighboring seasons and how to tell

True Winter sits between two seasons on the wheel, and the honest answer is usually found by trying the colors rather than by studying your veins.

Bright Winter — Shares your cool undertone and your clarity, but turns the volume up. A Bright Winter looks a little flat in plain black and white and comes alive when an electric blue or a hot pink is added; a True Winter is complete in black and white alone.

Deep Winter — Shares your cool side and your love of black, but the whole impression is darker and the undertone a touch more neutral, so a Deep Winter can carry a burgundy that would dull you. If icy pink looks thin on you rather than crisp, look here.

A three-question self-check

Do these in daylight, with a bare face if you can.

  • Put pure white and cream against your jaw. If cream looks dingy and white looks clean, you are on the winter side of the wheel.
  • Wear black and white with nothing else. If the outfit looks complete, you are likely a True Winter. If something seems missing, try Bright Winter.
  • Hold icy pink against your face. If it reads crisp, stay here. If it reads washed out, try Deep Winter.

Two out of three pointing the same way is enough to start shopping with.

Shopping notes

Search terms do more work than brand names. These are the words that surface your colors in most shops' filters and product titles.

  • For neutrals: jet black, optic white, bright white, charcoal, ink navy, slate grey.
  • For accents: true red, cherry red, cobalt, royal blue, fuchsia, magenta, emerald, ice pink, ice blue.
  • Words that usually mean a color is wrong for you: dusty, heather, marl, vintage wash, oatmeal, camel, honey, rust.
  • Fabric matters too: poplin, sateen, fine merino and leather show a pure color at its cleanest; brushed or slubby fabrics soften it toward grey.

When a listing photo is ambiguous, look at the product on a white background: if the item makes the white look yellow, it is cool enough for you.

Questions people ask

Can a True Winter wear brown at all?

A very dark, cool espresso worn with white will pass, but it will never be your best. What you probably want from brown is softness, and deep navy or charcoal give you that without the warmth.

Is silver the only metal I can wear?

Silver, platinum, white gold and polished steel are the easy answers. A very pale, cool-toned gold can work in small amounts; silver with black stones or clear crystal suits you better than yellow gold ever will.

I have dark skin. Does True Winter still apply?

Yes. The season describes undertone, clarity and contrast, not how light or dark the skin is. Deep brown skin with a cool cast and clear dark eyes is one of the most common True Winter combinations, and true red, emerald and pure white look extraordinary on it.

The quiz said True Winter but I feel washed out in icy colors. What now?

Icy pastels are the most fragile part of this palette. Keep them as a shirt or scarf under something dark, and if the whole pale end fails you consistently, read the Deep Winter page.
